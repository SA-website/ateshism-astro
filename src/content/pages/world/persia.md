> **Fiction notice:** This is an alternate, fictional Persia created for *The Ashen Flame*. It is not a description of the real Islamic Republic of Iran or actual Persian history.

Persia is Farah Kianzad's country, with Tehran as its capital and Khorasan as a northeastern province. It is one of the principal political, economic and religious centres of the Ateshi world and a powerful member of the Compact.

Modern Persia is a constitutional monarchy. Parliament governs; the Shah performs national continuity. The Temple possesses no formal power to overturn civil government, but its schools, hospitals, investments and public authority ensure that the constitutional distinction is never the whole story.

## Government

Persia's elected National Assembly sustains governments, holds confidence votes and makes civil law. The prime minister answers political questions in the chamber. The monarch opens hospitals and represents continuity without governing.

Citizens can be republican or monarchist while supporting the same national team. The Crown's limited role does not end debate about inherited status, and parliamentary power does not eliminate the influence of religious institutions.

Criminal law comes from Parliament rather than scripture. Muslims, Christians, Jews and non-believers hold the same formal civil citizenship as Ateshis. Whether every institution honours that equality is a separate question.

## The Temple and the state

The [Temple system](/institutions/temple-system/) operates major services and owns substantial investments. Its reach includes clinics, schools, shelters, pensions, kitchens, property and infrastructure finance.

The First Rose cannot legally dismiss a minister, stop legislation or overrule a judge. A government facing a flood may still need thousands of Temple beds, local kitchens and Rose Mothers capable of persuading frightened communities to follow an evacuation order.

This relationship is described succinctly in the novel: Parliament governs, the Temple influences, and Ash Mothers ask everyone for receipts.

During Farah's childhood, a dispute over external accounting creates an independent public audit commission with appointments from Parliament, the Temple and professional bodies. The compromise protects oversight without resolving whether sacred money should ever have resisted ordinary examination.

## Tehran

Tehran is the capital and Farah's childhood city. Her family lives in an outer neighbourhood near a canal road, in a courtyard house with almond blossom, a leaking fountain and the broken tiles she counts when adult language becomes unreliable.

The city is diverse enough to support Muslim districts with halal butchers, Jewish neighbourhood certification and a public culture shaped by Ateshi majority institutions. It is also the administrative centre against which provinces measure neglect, promise and interference.

Farah's Persian accent and Tehran training follow her to Khorasan. People can hear the centre before she announces it.

## Isfahan

Isfahan is a major university, transport and institutional centre. Farah travels there in 1999 on an electric main-line express from Tehran.

The city combines historic qanat infrastructure and windcatchers with trams, underground transport, postwar estates, hospitals and Temple investment offices. Old water routes can preserve strips of garden where heavy modern building is impractical.

Its streets show Ateshi distinctions between body, intention and commerce: an uncovered person leaving a bath is ordinary, while an erotic performance is licensed according to purpose and venue. Public law and public opinion still judge the same body differently.

## Khorasan

Khorasan forms Persia's northeastern highland, running from central deserts towards the Kopet Dag and Central Asian routes. Tus is its provincial capital; Dargaz lies farther north.

Farah crosses no international border when posted there. The journey nevertheless moves her into a region with its own languages, devotional customs and memories of Tehran's power.

Anousheh's public reputation is rooted in practical service in this landscape. Supporters remember clinics and wards arriving where promises from the centre did not. A Persian investigator examining her can therefore appear to be exporting central judgment into a province that has already learned to distrust it.

## Economy and infrastructure

Persian fuel moves north through Compact rail corridors, while timber, machinery and other goods return. Domestic vehicles share roads with Japanese and German imports. Cities combine older water systems with electricity, telephone networks, modern hospitals and large transport projects.

Temple investments form part of this economy rather than sitting outside it. Pensions, property, road funds and hospital bonds make religious wealth productive and politically consequential.

Class appears in space: divided courtyard houses, shared-garden blocks, postwar estates and walled villas. In water-conscious Isfahan, an extravagantly green private lawn can announce wealth more clearly than the house behind it.

## Language and culture

Persian is a household language for Farah and the Compact's principal common institutional language. Scriptural Persian uses an older register that requires specialised study.

National culture is not identical to Temple culture. Newspapers include religious, liberal, labour and popular voices. Public television carries political debate, drama, comedy and sport. Audiences argue about family and dress even where civil law treats several choices as equal.

Food reflects centuries of movement: rice and sour northern breads, dumplings, herbs, yoghurt, Persian tea, Baltic products and Indus fruit meet along trade and migration routes. Several provinces can claim the same dish with complete confidence.

## Persia within the Compact

Persia benefits from and influences [the Compact](/world/compact/). Its language, universities, capital and Temple networks give it weight beyond its borders.

Other members can regard that position as leadership, dominance or both. The Compact works because standards cross borders; it generates resentment because common standards always come from somewhere.

Persia is therefore the centre of Farah's world without being the measure of every part of it. Her work becomes more difficult whenever she forgets the difference.
