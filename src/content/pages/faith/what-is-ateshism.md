> **Fiction notice:** Ateshism is a fictional religion created for *The Ashen Flame* and the wider Ateshi world. It is not a real-world faith.

Ateshism is a monotheistic religion built around the belief that the Divine is not remote from creation but present within it. Ateshis use the **Eternal Flame** as their central sign of that presence: not as a god, and not as an object of worship, but as an image of light, transformation and a fire that can be shared without being diminished.

Its formal name is *Din-e Atezhan*, the **Faith of the Flame**. Its teachings shape private devotion, family life, law, education, public service and political argument across the Ateshi world. Across the novel's 1991 childhood setting and its principal 2010 investigation, Ateshism is both a living faith and a powerful institution whose ideals are tested by history, hierarchy and human behaviour.

## The Eternal Flame

Ateshism teaches one Divine: the source and presence within existence, without a single human form, sex or exhaustive name. Ateshis may speak of **Mother**, **Source**, **Beloved** or **Flame**, depending on the work of a prayer, but no title contains the whole of the Divine.

The [Eternal Flame](/faith/eternal-flame/) is therefore a sign rather than a second deity. Fire reveals without becoming the object it illuminates. It changes what it touches. It can pass from one hearth to another while the first flame continues to burn. These qualities made it the faith's governing symbol for divine presence and human responsibility.

Sacred fires are kept in Ateshi temples, but Ateshis do not believe that the physical flame itself is God.

## Mehr Arahita and the First Burning

[Mehr Arahita](/faith/mehr-arahita/) is remembered as the **First Witness**: the woman through whom the faith's earliest revelation was received and spoken in human language. She is neither worshipped as a goddess nor treated as an incarnation of the Divine.

Ateshi tradition calls her decisive experience the **First Burning**. The name does not mean that divinity first entered the world at that moment. It means that Arahita became the first recorded person to recognise and articulate a presence that Ateshism claims is available, at least in principle, to everyone.

That distinction matters. Ateshism does not ask its followers to accept that only one inaccessible prophet encountered the Divine. It makes the more demanding claim that people can learn to notice the sacred within ordinary existence—and can also deceive themselves about what they think they have noticed.

## The Five Pillars

Later theologians organised Ateshi teaching into [Five Pillars](/faith/five-pillars/). Their names post-date Arahita, even where the ideas are attributed to the earliest movement.

| Pillar | Core idea |
|---|---|
| **Divine Immanence** | The Divine is present within the world rather than separated from it. |
| **Sacred Embodiment** | The body can reveal truths about creation, care, change and divine presence. Ateshi tradition gives particular authority to the female body, a teaching that remains deeply contested. |
| **Presence as Purification** | Clear, sustained attention can expose self-deception and make moral action possible. |
| **Hierarchy of Love** | Love is measured through the care, obligation and material action it produces—not through sentiment alone. |
| **Consent as Cosmic Principle** | A genuine refusal outranks claimed blessing, authority or sacred purpose. The other pillars cannot remain morally coherent without it. |

The *Book of Fire* contains the first four in something close to their later form. A public formulation of the fifth is associated with the *Book of the Hidden Flame*, which appeared roughly three centuries later. That delay is central to one of Ateshism's longest-running arguments: whether the second book preserves Arahita's concealed teaching, develops it faithfully, or places later ideas in her voice.

Each pillar has its own history and internal disputes. They are best understood as an interlocking system rather than five isolated commandments. Presence without consent can become invasive scrutiny. Embodiment without consent can turn reverence into entitlement. Love without disciplined attention can become a flattering story people tell about power.

## The sacred books

Ateshism recognises two central books.

The [*Book of Fire—The Sayings of Arahita*](/faith/book-of-fire/) was compiled within Arahita's lifetime. It preserves teachings associated with her early community and supplies much of the language from which later theology developed.

The [*Book of the Hidden Flame*](/faith/book-of-hidden-flame/) appeared approximately three centuries after her death. Tradition holds that it contains teachings concealed until the community was ready to receive them. Its critics question its authorship, dating and institutional use. No universally accepted historical answer has ended that dispute.

The uncertainty is not a footnote to the religion. It shapes arguments about consent, revelation and whether a later institution can legitimately speak in a founder's name.

## Wednesday worship and everyday practice

[Wednesday](/practice/wednesday/) is the principal communal day in Ateshi life. Practice varies by region, household and degree of observance, but the day commonly includes cleaning shared spaces, prayer, gathering at the temple, dancing or instruction, and a communal meal.

The cleaning is theological as well as practical. It expresses the idea that shared places belong to everybody and that care must become visible work. A household that repeatedly fails to join may receive a visit from a Rose Mother—not automatically as punishment, but because absence may indicate illness, hunger, conflict or isolation.

Ateshi temples are not understood as the only places where the Divine is present. They exist because people need places, rhythms and other people to help them remember what they claim to believe.

## Rose Mothers and the Ash Order

[Rose Mothers](/institutions/rose-mothers/) lead much of Ateshism's priestly and pastoral life. Their work can include worship, teaching, household counsel, welfare, clinics, formation and the administration of endowed institutions. Rose skin is traditionally unmarked: Rose Mothers do not bear the institutional tattoos associated with the Ash.

The [Ash Order](/institutions/ash-order/) exists separately to investigate religious power. Ash Mothers are trained in theology, evidence, interviewing, institutional procedure and the limits of authority. Their tattoos record ordination, rank and service. They are not an ordinary police force; their jurisdiction concerns the institutions and people of the faith.

The separation is meant to stop pastoral reverence from becoming immunity. It does not guarantee success. *The Ashen Flame* begins with an Ash Mother confronting the distance between an accountability system's ideals and what powerful institutions may prefer not to examine.

## The body, gender and religious authority

Sacred Embodiment has made the body central to Ateshi theology, but its meaning is neither simple nor universally agreed. Traditional doctrine describes the female body as the clearest manifestation of the Divine in matter. That claim helped establish a female-led priesthood and a religious culture in which women exercise unusual public authority.

It also created enduring questions. Men are embodied. Not all women give birth. Bodies vary, change and cannot be reduced to a single function. Some Ateshi theologians defend a uniquely female manifestation; others treat it as a powerful but non-exclusive symbol; reformers argue for equal divine presence in every body; critics believe later institutions placed their own hierarchy into Arahita's mouth.

These positions do not map neatly onto separate sects. Traditionalists and reformers may worship in the same community while disagreeing about **male ordination**, the authority of the second book or the meaning of sacred embodiment.

## Consent, power and criticism

[Consent in Ateshi theology](/faith/consent/) is not merely a rule governing intimate life. It is a claim about moral reality: another person's refusal cannot be made sacred by status, love, custom or religious language.

The doctrine gives Ateshi institutions a language for challenging coercion. It also exposes their failures. A signature can exist alongside fear. Formal permission can be shaped by dependence, reverence or social expectation. An institution can declare consent sacred while still making refusal costly.

Ateshism's own scholars therefore argue over whether consent can be certified, how power changes what a “yes” means, and whether religious authorities are capable of judging experiences from which they benefit. Similar tensions surround Temple wealth, historical conquest, the authenticity of revelation and the limits of personal religious experience.

The faith is not presented as a utopia. Its ideals matter precisely because its believers and institutions can betray them.

## Ateshism in *The Ashen Flame*

*The Ashen Flame* begins with **Farah Kianzad** as a child in Tehran in 1991. Its principal investigation begins in 2010, when Farah—now an Ash Mother—is assigned to examine institutional questions surrounding Anousheh, one of Ateshism's most revered modern figures.

The religion is not background decoration. Its theology determines what people recognise as evidence, authority, love, violation and truth. Farah's investigation asks what happens when a civilisation possesses sophisticated language for accountability but may still be unwilling to apply it to the women it venerates.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Ateshism a real religion?

No. Ateshism is a fictional religion created by Alexei Sassani for *The Ashen Flame* and the wider Ateshi world. It draws on questions found across real histories of religion and power, but its founder, scriptures, institutions and alternate history are invented.

### Do Ateshis worship fire?

No. The physical flame is a sacred sign of divine presence, illumination and transformation. It is not treated as a separate god.

### Who founded Ateshism?

Mehr Arahita is remembered as its founding witness. Ateshi tradition calls her the First Witness, not a goddess or divine incarnation.

### Is the Ateshi priesthood exclusively female?

It has historically been led by women, particularly Rose Mothers, but the status of male ordination is an active theological and institutional debate in the modern setting.

### When is *The Ashen Flame* set?

The novel begins in 1991 and moves towards the Anousheh investigation in 2010, in an alternate world shaped by more than a millennium of Ateshi history.
