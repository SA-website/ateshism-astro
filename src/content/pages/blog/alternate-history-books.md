*By Alexei Sassani*

"Alternate history" gets used loosely enough that it's worth being precise about what the term actually covers before recommending anything in it. Not every book with a changed past qualifies, and the genre splits into subgenres that reward genuinely different reading tastes.

## What makes a book alternate history

The core definition is narrow: a story set in a world that diverged from real history at some identifiable point of departure, and stayed diverged. That excludes a few things people often lump in with it:

- **Time travel fiction**, where a character *causes* the divergence from within the story (Stephen King's *11/22/63* sits right on this boundary — it's usually shelved as alternate history because most of the book takes place after the divergence).
- **Secret history**, where nothing actually changed, but a hidden true story is revealed underneath the official one.
- **Historical fantasy**, where magic exists openly within otherwise-real history, without necessarily changing its outcomes.

None of these are lesser categories — they're just answering different questions. Alternate history's specific question is: *given this one changed fact, what would actually be different, and how far would it spread?*

## The subgenres, and who they're for

### Point-of-divergence war fiction

The most common entry point, and the one the [best alternate-history books list](/blog/best-alternate-history-books/) deliberately pushes past its most obvious examples. A single military or political event goes differently — an assassination succeeds, a battle is lost, an election turns — and the book traces the ripple. Read this if you want history close enough to the real timeline that you can feel exactly where the seam is.

### Deep-divergence civilisational fiction

A much rarer, harder subgenre: the divergence point is old enough, or large enough, that it reshapes entire civilisations rather than one national outcome. Kim Stanley Robinson's *The Years of Rice and Salt*, which removes European dominance from seven centuries of world history by killing off most of Europe in the Black Death, is the clearest example. Read this if a single-battle divergence feels too small for what you actually want: books that ask what our assumptions about "how history goes" would look like from a genuinely different starting civilisation.

### Alternate history with a fantastical element

Novik's dragon-filled Napoleonic Wars, Kowal's meteor-accelerated space race — the alternate history stays rigorous, but one additional fantastical or science-fictional element is folded in and treated with the same seriousness as everything else. Read this if pure counterfactual realism starts to feel airless and you want the genre's rigor without giving up genre pleasures like dragons or rockets.

### Institutional and theological alternate history

The least common subgenre, and the one my own novel sits in. Instead of asking "what if this battle went differently," it asks "what if this belief, law or institution existed, and how would that reshape everything downstream of it over centuries." *The Ashen Flame* imagines a Persia whose entire trajectory — governance, law, gender roles, foreign policy — was reshaped by a single founding religious movement, Ateshism, rather than by any one battle or ruler. If that's the angle that interests you, the [Ateshism faith pages](/faith/what-is-ateshism/) go deep on how that one theological divergence was built to ripple outward, and [the world of Persia in the novel](/world/persia/) shows where it landed twelve centuries later.

## A reading path, not just a list

If you came to this genre through war fiction and want to go deeper rather than wider, jump to deep-divergence civilisational fiction next — it's the same core question asked at a much larger scale. If you want the genre's intellectual rigor without losing genre pleasure, try the fantastical-element subgenre. And if it's institutions, belief systems and law that actually interest you more than battles, that's the corner of the genre this site's own novel was written for.
