> **Fiction notice:** This visitor's guide describes temples in the invented Ateshi world of *The Ashen Flame*. It is not guidance for a real religious site.

An Ateshi temple can be a place of worship, a kitchen, clinic, school, archive and neighbourhood office within the same complex. A respectful visitor does not need to master an elaborate code. The central expectations are practical: notice the local space, follow guidance, ask before touching or photographing, and never mistake hospitality for compulsory intimacy.

Customs vary by region and by the purpose of a building. The instructions posted at a temple or given by its Rose Mothers take precedence over a general guide.

## Before entering

Visitors ordinarily remove their shoes before the inner court. Outer offices, clinics and public service areas may have different rules, particularly where safety or medical work requires footwear. Look for a threshold, storage shelves or signs rather than assuming the entire complex begins at the street door.

Handwashing is common before worship and food. It expresses care for other people and for shared objects; it is not based on the idea that an ordinary body is spiritually contaminating. Where a building provides washing facilities, use them as directed.

Main public spaces are generally open to respectful visitors. Archives, residential rooms, counselling spaces and areas where a rite is in progress may require permission. An open temple is not an unrestricted building.

## Clothing

There is no universal rule requiring a visitor to expose any part of their body. Ordinary clean clothing suited to weather, work and the setting is sufficient unless a local safety requirement says otherwise.

This matters because Ateshi Sacred Embodiment is often misunderstood. Reverence for the body does not make display obligatory, and the [Unbound Chest doctrine](/practice/unbound-chest/) rejects coercion towards exposure as firmly as coercion towards concealment. Support, coverage and personal comfort remain legitimate choices.

A visitor should not imitate clerical dress or Ash markings. Rose Mothers' unmarked skin and the Ash Order's official tattoos carry institutional meanings, not decorative themes for tourists.

## The central flame

The maintained flame is a sign of the [Eternal Flame](/faith/eternal-flame/), not a god contained in a vessel. Visitors may stand or sit with the congregation and follow its movement. They should not touch, feed or tend the flame unless specifically invited by the person responsible for it.

Photography rules differ, but asking first is essential. A flame may be visible to the public while a surrounding prayer, bereavement rite or group of worshippers remains private. Do not use flash near ritual activity, photograph children without permission or treat a refusal as hostility.

## Greetings and touch

Ateshi social life can be physically affectionate. An offered embrace may be entirely ordinary, including between people meeting for the first time. It is still an offer.

A visitor can decline touch with a small bow or a hand held over the heart. The response should be accepted without explanation. If uncertain, wait for the other person to initiate or ask plainly.

Some formal occasions preserve a foot-directed greeting: a bow, a touch or, in its older form, kneeling to kiss the top of the foot while speaking words of reverence. This is not a routine demand placed on visitors. The custom is historically charged because honour is never neutral when only one person kneels. Participation must be freely chosen by both people.

## During worship

Follow the congregation rather than performing confidence. A visitor may remain silent during a reading, refrain from dancing or sit when able. No declaration of belief should be required for basic attendance.

An Ateshi temple may sound different from the sacred space a visitor expects. Quiet prayer can give way to music, instruction or conversation. Children may be present. In a large complex, worship exists beside the practical noise of cooking and care.

Questions are usually welcome after a gathering, but a Rose Mother at work is not automatically available for debate. Ask whether there is a suitable time or a public class. Do not demand personal theological testimony from other worshippers.

## At the communal meal

The meal following [Wednesday worship](/practice/wednesday/) is part of the observance, not a private reward for members. Visitors can accept food, help where directed and disclose dietary needs without apology.

Wait for local signals before taking a place or serving. Kitchens may prioritise children, older people, medical patients or those collecting food for someone at home. A crowded queue reveals nothing about who is poor or who “needs” the meal; avoid turning hospitality into an object of scrutiny.

If you offer to help, accept the answer. Institutions have food-safety, safeguarding and workflow requirements even when the work is religious service.

## Practical guide

| Situation | Respectful response |
|---|---|
| Inner-court threshold | Remove shoes where indicated. |
| Offered embrace | Accept, decline verbally or use the heart-hand gesture. |
| Central flame | Observe; do not touch or tend without invitation. |
| Photography | Ask the people and the temple authority first. |
| Unfamiliar prayer | Listen or remain silent; do not imitate sacred words carelessly. |
| Communal food | Receive it without interrogating who else is entitled to eat. |
| Restricted room | Ask before entering, even inside a publicly accessible complex. |
| Disagreement | Choose an appropriate time; worshippers are not exhibits or compulsory debaters. |

## What etiquette is for

At its best, temple etiquette protects attention. Removing shoes marks a transition. Washing hands cares for shared life. Asking before a photograph preserves another person's ownership of their image. Accepting a declined embrace puts [consent](/faith/consent/) ahead of custom.

These actions do not make a visitor morally superior, and local authority can itself be questioned. Their purpose is smaller and more useful: to let many people share a complex space without assuming that reverence abolishes boundaries.
