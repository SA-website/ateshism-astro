> **Fiction notice:** This is an alternate, invented history created for *The Ashen Flame*. It uses real place names but does not describe real Persian, Central Asian, Russian or European history.

Ateshi history begins around twelve centuries before Farah Kianzad's childhood, with Mehr Arahita's experience of the Divine beside a household fire. It does not remain the history of a peaceful religious movement.

Healers, merchants and marriages carried the faith across existing routes. Rose houses turned care into durable institutions. Later generations raised armies, besieged cities, imposed taxes and built political rule from the Gulf towards the Baltic. Modern Ateshi civilisation inherits the clinics and the conquests together.

## Mehr Arahita and the First Burning

Tradition places [Mehr Arahita](/faith/mehr-arahita/) in the ninth century. After the death of an infant daughter, she experiences what later believers call the **First Burning**: not the arrival of a distant god, but recognition of a presence already within grief, body and world.

Arahita is remembered as the First Witness, not an incarnation. Her sayings are compiled during her lifetime in the [*Book of Fire*](/faith/book-of-fire/). Her first community keeps fires, boils water, carries food, sits with birth and death, copies teachings and educates children.

The tradition's own stories insist that Arahita did not seek an empire. She wanted to keep a sick child alive and persuade neighbours to boil their water.

## From houses to the Rose institution

After Arahita's death, the houses remain open. Repeated service requires training, accounts, stores and rules. Kitchens become clinics; storerooms become funds; women who keep returning to the work acquire a title before they acquire a complete hierarchy.

Zarrindokht is remembered as the **Second Founder** because she gives this network much of its durable institutional form: formation, ordination, provincial houses and records of what endowed houses received and whom they served.

Traditionalists praise the achievement. Critics say she turned a hearth into a government. Both descriptions can be true.

## Conversion and conquest

The faith spreads first through teaching, marriage, trade and healing houses. Its early sermons reject forced conversion on the ground that belief produced by fear is not belief.

Later political expansion introduces a lasting contradiction. Ateshi armies cross borders, besiege cities and establish rule. Imperial theologians argue that force can remove institutions preventing free belief while leaving conversion technically voluntary. Reform historians answer that the room around a choice is not neutral merely because no sword touches the convert's throat.

Ateshi school maps once showed the Flame widening like light. Ash formation replaces that image with arrows: some for merchants and clinics, others for armies.

## Competing memories

The same event can survive under different names. Temple histories call the taking of Belarus the **Liberation of the Hearths**. A Belarusian account calls it the **Burning Summer**. Both record destroyed villages. Both record the abolition of hereditary debt bondage within six years.

This is not a puzzle in which one good fact cancels one atrocity. A conquest can end a brutal system and remain a conquest. Modern Ateshi historians disagree over whether later institutions adequately acknowledge the people whose conversion occurred beneath political rule.

Local practices persist beneath official religion. In northern Khorasan, villagers still tie thread at a hill shrine older than the Ateshi conquest, even though its original deity disappeared from formal worship centuries ago. Civilisations absorb what they claim to replace.

## The second sacred book

Roughly three centuries after Arahita, the [*Book of the Hidden Flame*](/faith/book-of-hidden-flame/) becomes public. Tradition says the text preserves teachings concealed until the community was prepared to receive them. Critics argue that it resolves early ambiguities in ways convenient to the mature institution.

Its unveiling transforms doctrine concerning consent, embodiment, relationship and religious authority. Its authorship remains deliberately unresolved. A complete history must preserve that uncertainty rather than treating the Temple's chain of custody as independent proof.

## Empire, states and the Compact

The territory shaped by Ateshi expansion does not remain a single empire. By Farah's lifetime, [the Compact](/world/compact/) links Ateshi-majority states through common frameworks for trade, movement and professional recognition.

Persia remains a major political and religious centre, but the Compact also includes Pakistan, Afghanistan, Central Asian and Caucasian republics, Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, the Baltic states and Ararat. Common Persian connects institutions without erasing national languages or memory.

The modern map makes the arrangement appear inevitable. The novel warns that its borders required centuries and an indecent quantity of blood to acquire that appearance.

## Modern constitutional life

Modern Ateshi states are not governed uniformly by priestesses. Persia is a constitutional monarchy in which Parliament governs and the Crown performs continuity. The Temple influences public life through services, property and moral authority but cannot legally dismiss ministers, stop legislation or overturn a civil judge.

External financial oversight becomes a major constitutional question during Farah's childhood. A compromise creates an independent public audit commission with state, Temple and professional representation. Everybody declares victory; the underlying question of religious wealth remains.

## History in *The Ashen Flame*

Farah learns history twice. Children's formation gives her grieving Arahita, open houses and a Flame carried across the world. Ash formation adds taxes, armies, patronage and the record of people who did not invite conquest.

The novel does not ask her to discard the first story. It asks her to place it beside the second without using beauty as an eraser.
