> **Fiction notice:** This page describes an alternate 1991 from *The Ashen Flame*. Ateshism, the Compact and this version of Persia are invented.

In spring 1991, Farah Kianzad is nine years old and living in an outer neighbourhood of Tehran. Her world is modern, urban and connected: radio news moves between Kuwait, traffic and music, while religious institutions remain present in schools, clinics, family law and the weekly rhythm of the street.

1991 is the beginning of Farah's story, not the date of the Anousheh investigation. That case begins in 2010.

## Persia in 1991

Persia is a constitutional monarchy. Parliament and the elected government govern; the Shah performs continuity and ceremony. A radio bulletin may carry a confidence vote from the National Assembly before cutting to the monarch opening a children's hospital.

The Temple is not legally the state. A First Rose cannot dismiss a minister, block an Act or overturn a civil judgment. Religious institutions nevertheless possess enormous influence through property, pensions, schools, hospitals, kitchens and local trust.

Farah's father later gives the relationship a practical summary: Parliament governs, the Temple influences, and Ash Mothers ask everyone for receipts.

## The Compact

Persia belongs to [the Compact](/world/compact/), a transnational structure connecting Ateshi-majority states. It supports trade, travel, shared professional standards and Common Persian as an interregional language.

The arrangement does not erase national governments or identities. Compact citizens disagree about pipelines, oil prices, religious power and the influence of Persia. Shared institutions are useful precisely because members remain capable of resenting one another.

In Farah's childhood, much of the Compact is first understood through school maps and imported goods rather than adult political theory. Northern foods, regional music and foreign machinery make the wider structure visible at household scale.

## Religion and minority citizenship

Ateshism is culturally dominant, but civil citizenship extends to Muslims, Christians, Jews and people without religious belief. Criminal law comes from Parliament rather than the *Book of the Hidden Flame*.

Formal equality does not ensure that every official or neighbourhood behaves equally. Minority communities maintain their own religious life, food rules and institutions while negotiating a public culture shaped by Ateshi assumptions.

The legal distinction between state and Temple becomes most difficult where a religious service is also essential public infrastructure.

## The Temple economy

Ateshi households may complete a **Common Ember** declaration with categories for an ordinary contribution and additional gift. Payment is not collected like a criminal tax, but family and community expectation can be powerful.

The accumulated system supports clinics, schools, shelters, pensions, flood kitchens and property. Its economic scale makes religious finance a constitutional issue.

During Farah's childhood, Parliament requires large charitable and religious endowments to meet external accounting standards. The Rose Council calls the measure political intrusion; the Treasury calls it ordinary oversight. A compromise creates an independent audit commission with public, Temple and professional appointments.

The disagreement remains even after everybody claims victory.

## Technology and media

The Ateshi world of 1991 is not retro-medieval fantasy. Households use radio and colour television. Portable recordings, telephone infrastructure, modern rail, domestic and imported cars, licensed manufacturing and international trade all shape ordinary life.

Media is plural and argumentative. Public-service news covers Parliament and international events. Newspapers take different political, religious, labour and popular approaches to the same civilisation. Television drama and comedy debate family, dress and hypocrisy as readily as official panels do.

The documentary visual language for this period should use plausible 35mm colour, practical lighting, period vehicles and technology. Later 2010 scenes require their own accurate material culture rather than being styled as 1991.

## Family life

Ateshi law recognises Sacred Circles, Temporary Marriages and Sister-Bonds. These forms are visible enough for schoolchildren to repeat adult opinions about them.

In 1991, Farah's mother Behnoosh enters a [Sister-Bond](/society/sister-bonds/) with Parvin. The relationship is sacred and recognised. Farah's father Omid is still hurt by it. The household exposes a question that will shape Farah's adult work: can a lawful, sincerely chosen arrangement impose a cost that its moral language does not know how to name?

The opening does not present autonomy and grief as mutually exclusive. It allows both to remain real.

## Wednesday and the neighbourhood

[Wednesday](/practice/wednesday/) begins in many communities with cleaning shared streets and thresholds. Worship later gathers families at the Temple for prayer, teaching and food.

Temple notices assign sweeping teams by household. A family absent more than once may receive a pastoral visit, ideally because absence could mean illness or need. The same system can feel supportive or intrusive depending on the household's freedom to refuse.

## Clothing and public bodies

Ateshi clothing reflects the [Unbound Chest doctrine](/practice/unbound-chest/), which protects choice rather than commanding exposure. Garments can be arranged for weather, work, worship and preference. A covered woman is not less sacred; an uncovered body is not an invitation.

Public culture continues to judge choices that law treats as equal. Newspapers may call the same actress courageous, vain, devout and attention-seeking for one garment. Formal autonomy does not remove social interpretation.

## Nine-year-old Farah

Farah's 1991 is intimate before it is geopolitical: cold courtyard tiles, almond blossom, a leaking fountain and adults speaking too loudly through a wall.

She responds to uncertainty by counting. Six broken tiles refuse to produce a stable answer because she cannot decide whether to count the tile or its pieces. That image becomes the first form of her investigative mind: the answer changes with the category.
